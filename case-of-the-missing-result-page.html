The Case of the Missing Result Page

A Computer Networks detective comedy covering Units III, IV and V

Part 1: The Prologue and The Nameless Device

Prologue: 10 p.m. in the Orange City

It was 10 p.m. in Nagpur, the Orange City, and the semester results were out. Aarav Deshmukh had been sitting cross-legged on his bedroom floor, staring at his phone since eight, with his laptop open beside him and his tea gone cold.

The group chat was already on fire.

Meera: Bro are you in?? I passed everything, I think I’m crying

Kunal: Loaded in 2 seconds. Easy. Some of us were born for this

Mom: Aarav, why are you not replying? Have you seen it?

Aarav took a deep breath and tapped the result link. The page spun. And spun. Then, in cold grey letters: This site can’t be reached.

“KUNAL got through,” he whispered. “Kunal thinks DHCP is a biscuit.”

He jabbed at the refresh button again. And again. “It’s a server problem. Honestly, it’s probably… ”

He stopped. The router on the shelf had blinked once, twice, and a tiny figure in a trench coat had climbed out of the light. On his shoulder sat a green parrot.

“Inspector Packet, network detective,” said the figure, tipping his hat. “I live inside data packets. This is my partner, Ping.”

“Ping!” squawked the parrot.

“Pong,” said the Inspector, without turning his head. “He repeats everything. You get used to it.”

Aarav stared. “I have been awake for nineteen hours. I am not hallucinating a parrot.”

“Parrot!” said Ping.

“…Okay, I’m hallucinating a parrot. Hypothesis noted. I’ll test it.”

“Test away,” said the Inspector. “Now listen. A request has a long way to travel, and it can fail at four places. Either you never properly joined the network, or the packet got lost on the way, or the destination is in trouble, or somebody has shut the door. We check them in order. Our motto: follow the packet.”

“What about my earbuds?” Aarav asked. “They’re connected to my phone.”

“Bluetooth? Different club, ten metres wide. Ping, cross them off.”

“Crossed off!” said Ping.

Case File: Bluetooth. Bluetooth is a short-range wireless technology (roughly ten metres). One master device connects with a few slaves (up to seven active) in a small network called a piconet. It plays no part in reaching a website over the Internet.

Chapter 1: The Nameless Device (Unit III)

“Clue number one,” said the Inspector, shrinking both of them to the size of a coin and sliding them into the router. “Who are you? On the Internet, you’re nobody until somebody gives you an address.”

Inside the router stood a hotel lobby. Behind the desk was Mrs. DHCP, a brisk receptionist with a lanyard and a rack of room keys marked 192.168.1.x.

“When your phone first joined the Wi-Fi,” whispered the Inspector, “it had no address. So it did what any lost guest does. It shouted to the whole lobby.”

He replayed the memory. Discover! shouted the phone. Does anybody have a room for me? Mrs. DHCP looked up. Offer: “Room 192.168.1.23, sir.” Request: “Yes please, I’ll take it!” Acknowledge: “Lovely. It’s yours for the next few hours, then the lease is up. Don’t trash it.”

“Why all the dots and numbers?” asked Aarav.

“That’s an IPv4 address,” said Mrs. DHCP, stamping a form. “Thirty-two bits, four numbers. The world is nearly out of rooms in this hotel.”

“Which is why IPv6 exists,” said Aarav, remembering his lecture notes. “128 bits. Enough rooms for every phone, fridge and doorbell on Earth.”

“Correct,” sniffed Mrs. DHCP, “and half the world still queues at my old hotel anyway.”

But a room number wasn’t enough. To hand anything to the router next door, the phone needed its hardware (MAC) address. So the phone became a town crier and bellowed across the lobby: “WHO HAS 192.168.1.1? TELL ME YOUR MAC ADDRESS!”

Everyone ignored him except the router, a bored security guard, who sighed. “That’s me. Here’s my MAC address. Stop shouting.” The phone wrote it in a notebook (the ARP cache) so he would never have to shout again.

“Why does a phone need two addresses?” Aarav muttered.

“Your IP address is where you live, and it changes when you move,” said the Inspector. “Your MAC address is the name stitched on your shirt. Address, check. Neighbour, check. You are definitely on the network. Ping?”

“Alibi!” said Ping, who had misunderstood the entire case.

Case File: Unit III, Part 1

DHCP gives a device its IP address automatically in four steps: Discover, Offer, Request, Acknowledge (DORA), with a lease time.

IPv4 addresses are 32 bits. IPv6 addresses are 128 bits.

ARP maps an IP address to a MAC address. The request is broadcast, the reply goes straight to the asker and is stored in the ARP cache.

RARP (MAC to IP) and BOOTP (the ancestor of DHCP) are older protocols that DHCP largely replaced.

Part 2: The Wrong Turn and The Handshake

Chapter 2: The Wrong Turn (Unit III)

“Clue number two: the journey,” said the Inspector, and they zoomed out of the router into the huge maze of the ISP’s network.

Every junction had a router, and every router asked the same question about every packet: where to next? Picking the road for each packet is forwarding. Drawing the signboards in the first place is routing. Three traffic officers were arguing about exactly that.

Rip Uncle (the RIP protocol) was the neighbourhood gossip. He counted every step to a place, refused to believe anywhere was more than 15 steps away, and every half minute told his neighbours everything he knew whether they asked or not. “Simple! Friendly! A bit slow to hear news!”

“Sixteen steps means unreachable,” Aarav whispered, flipping through his memory of the notes.

“Show-off,” said the Inspector.

“Show-off!” said Ping.

Ms. OSPF had the entire colony map pinned on her wall. She knew every road and every closure, ran Dijkstra’s shortest-path algorithm in her head, and picked the cheapest route inside one organisation’s network. “Road closed? Give me one second,” she said, and redrew everything.

Mr. BGP wore a suit and worked between states, which networks call autonomous systems. “Between states, the shortest road hardly matters,” he said smoothly. “What matters is who has an agreement with whom.” That explained a suspicious detour through another ISP’s network. The Inspector narrowed his eyes. Ping narrowed his too.

“Politics,” said the Inspector at last. “Annoying, not criminal. Every hop answered, and the packet reached the university’s front gate safely. The road is innocent.”

“So where is the problem?” asked Aarav.

The Inspector pointed to a huge glowing building with a queue that went around the block. “Inside.”

Case File: Unit III, Part 2

Forwarding is the per-packet decision using the routing table. Routing is the process that builds the table.

RIP is a distance-vector protocol that uses hop count (maximum 15).

OSPF is a link-state protocol that uses Dijkstra’s algorithm and link cost inside one autonomous system.

BGP is a path-vector protocol used between autonomous systems and is policy-driven.

Chapter 3: The Handshake (Unit IV)

“Welcome to Result Bhavan,” said the Inspector. “One address, thousands of rooms. We call the room numbers ports. Secure websites live in room 443, and DNS, mail and everything else have their own rooms.”

Outside room 443 sat a very tired clerk. Before anything could be exchanged, he demanded the full ceremony of the three-way handshake.

“SYN,” said Aarav’s phone politely. Hello, can we talk?

“SYN-ACK,” said the clerk, after what felt like a week. Yes. Can you hear me?

“ACK,” said the phone. Loud and clear.

“That’s a lot of ceremony just to say hello,” said Aarav.

“That’s TCP,” said the Inspector. “A registered courier: it makes sure both sides are ready, delivers everything in order, and chases whatever goes missing. The alternative is UDP, a postcard. Fast and carefree, and nobody checks if it arrived.”

“Like my video call with Nani tonight,” said Aarav slowly. “Her face kept glitching, but it never stopped. It just skipped.”

“Exactly. Resending an old video frame would only make her late, so UDP lets it go. But an exam result with a missing digit is a disaster. That’s why this needs the courier.”

“And when we’re done?” asked Aarav.

“Hanging up is polite too. One side says FIN, the other says ACK, and then the other side says its own FIN. Each direction is closed separately, like two people saying ‘you hang up first’.”

But tonight, the SYN-ACK was crawling. The clerk wasn’t broken. He was drowning. Behind them, in a line that stretched all the way back down Wardha Road, stood forty thousand students, all holding phones, all pressing refresh.

Case File: Unit IV, Part 1

Transport layer addressing uses port numbers (for example 443 for HTTPS, 80 for HTTP, 53 for DNS).

TCP connection establishment is the three-way handshake: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK.

TCP connection release uses FIN and ACK in each direction.

TCP is reliable and connection-oriented. UDP is connectionless with lower overhead and suits real-time traffic like video calls.

Part 3: The Traffic Jam and The Lost Address Book

Chapter 4: The Traffic Jam (Unit IV)

“If you want to know what forty thousand students do to a road,” said the Inspector, “meet TCP’s driving instructor.”

A nervous learner driver had to figure out how fast to go without knowing how crowded the road was. Rule one: slow start. Send one car. Then two. Then four, then eight, doubling every trip while all goes well.

“That’s not slow, that’s explosive,” said Aarav.

“It’s slow compared to what it could have done. It started with one.”

At a certain threshold, the driver switches to congestion avoidance and adds only one extra car per trip. And the moment cars go missing, he hits the brakes: if only a few replies are missing, he halves his speed; if there’s total silence, he goes back to one car and starts all over.

“Increase gently, brake hard,” said Aarav. “AIMD. It keeps the road fair for everyone. I remember this one from the lecture.”

“You’re basically quoting the textbook,” said the Inspector.

Aarav’s Wi-Fi blinked for a second. “Hold on, my connection just dropped!”

“TCP noticed,” said the Inspector. “Anything not acknowledged is sent again, so nothing gets lost. TCP doesn’t give up. TCP is not Kunal.”

“And if the server itself crashes and restarts?” asked Aarav.

“It wakes up with amnesia. When your phone says ‘as I was saying…’, the server replies ‘Who are you?’ That’s a reset, and the applications have to begin the conversation again.”

Aarav’s phone buzzed. Mom: AARAV.

Aarav frowned. “Wait. If the whole road is jammed, everybody should be slow. But Kunal loaded in two seconds.”

“Exactly,” said the Inspector, his eyes narrowing. “A traffic jam slows down everyone, not only you. Somebody is lying to us, and I smell old paper.”

Case File: Unit IV, Part 2

Flow control protects the receiver. Congestion control protects the network.

Slow start doubles the congestion window each round trip, then congestion avoidance adds one segment per round trip.

Three duplicate ACKs trigger fast retransmit and a halved window. A timeout resets the window to one segment.

After a host crash, connection state is lost, so the peer is reset and the application must recover.

Chapter 5: The Lost Address Book (Unit V)

The Inspector led him into the world’s largest, dustiest library: the DNS.

“You typed a name, not a number,” he said. “Computers can’t post letters to names. Somebody has to look the address up.”

Aarav’s phone had asked the ISP’s helpful clerk, the resolver. The clerk asked the Root office, who said, “No idea, try the .in office.” The .in office said, “Not us, try the university’s own office.” The university’s own server finally gave the real address. “Normally on UDP, port 53,” said the Inspector. “It’s a quick question, no ceremony needed.”

The clerk scribbled the answer on a sticky note and stuck it on the phone with an expiry time, the TTL. “Don’t ask again until it expires,” he said.

Aarav suddenly went very still. “I opened the portal at eight o’clock tonight to check for the notification. Kunal didn’t.”

“Go on,” said the Inspector, smiling.

“My sticky note is old. If the address changed after eight…”

“At 9 p.m.,” said the Inspector, flipping through his notebook, “the university moved the result portal to a bigger server with a new address, and Dynamic DNS updated the official address book at once. Like changing your phone number and having every contact update itself. But your sticky note still says the old address. You’ve been knocking on the old, exhausted door all evening. Kunal opened the portal for the first time at 10 p.m., with no sticky note, so he got the new address.”

“Show-off!” said Ping, to Aarav.

“…That one was fair,” Aarav admitted.

Aarav switched airplane mode on and off, which cleared his sticky note. His phone asked again and got the new address. Down the corridor, a clerk from the exam cell was uploading the results to the new server using FTP, which has two windows: one for talking (port 21) and one for passing the parcels (port 20).

“We’re closer,” said the Inspector. “But look.” The page began to load, froze, and then: Connection timed out.

“The sticky note was a suspect,” he said. “Not the culprit. The culprit is guarding the door.”

Case File: Unit V, Part 1

DNS translates domain names to IP addresses through a hierarchy: root, top-level domain, authoritative server.

Caching with a TTL speeds things up but can serve stale answers.

Dynamic DNS updates records automatically when an IP address changes.

FTP transfers files using a control connection (port 21) and a data connection (port 20).

Part 4: The Gatekeeper and The Verdict

Chapter 6: The Gatekeeper (Unit V)

At the gate of the new server stood Firewall Sharma-ji, a stern watchman with a moustache, a lathi and a very thick rulebook. He checked every packet’s sender, destination and port.

“Name?” he said to Aarav’s request. “Sender address? Hmm. Rule 47. Not allowed.”

“Sharma-ji, he’s a student!” said the Inspector.

“Rules are rules. Rule 47 was added at 9:55 p.m.: block everything from this range of addresses. There was a flood of suspicious traffic, so the admin blocked a whole neighbourhood. Your ISP lives in that neighbourhood.” He tapped his rulebook. “I do not make the rules. I follow them very, very loyally.”

“That rule is far too broad,” Aarav argued. “You’re blocking thousands of innocent students to stop a few attackers.”

“Rules,” repeated Sharma-ji, “are rules.”

“And that’s why Kunal got in,” said Aarav slowly. “He’s on a different ISP, outside Rule 47, and he had no sticky note. Case explained.”

“The culprit,” breathed the Inspector. “A good rule, written in a panic.”

Up in the control tower sat the sweating network admin. In the old days, fixing this would have meant running from switch to switch with a laptop and a samosa, changing fifty devices one by one. But the university used SDN, Software-Defined Networking.

“So the controller is the manager, and the switches are the workers?” asked Aarav.

“Precisely,” said the Inspector. “The thinking part, the control plane, lives in one central controller. The switches only move packets, the data plane, and obey the controller through a protocol such as OpenFlow.”

With one click, the admin narrowed Rule 47 to the real attackers and rerouted part of the crowd to a second server. Sharma-ji lowered his lathi. “Next!”

Aarav’s request went through, carrying an HTTP GET for the result page. The server replied 200 OK, the happiest status on the web. (The others you’ll meet are 404, for ‘not found’, and 503, for ‘I’m swamped, try later’.) The result page loaded.

Aarav read it and let out a huge breath. His phone buzzed.

Mom: WELL???

Aarav: Passed. Everything. Mom, I’ll explain later, it involves a parrot.

He looked at the router on the shelf and added, quietly, “And I owe a parrot an apology.”

Case File: Unit V, Part 2

A firewall filters packets using rules on addresses, ports and protocols. A rule that is too broad blocks legitimate users.

SDN separates the control plane (central controller) from the data plane (switches), which makes the network programmable.

HTTP is the request-response protocol of the WWW. Common status codes: 200 OK, 404 Not Found, 503 Service Unavailable.

Epilogue: The Verdict

Back on the shelf, the Inspector lined up the suspects. Uncle Stale, the old sticky note, had sent Aarav to the wrong door. The Crowd, the congestion, had slowed down everyone. Sharma-ji, the over-eager firewall rule, had shut the final door. Three suspects, three part-time culprits, and not a single villain.

“The layers all did their jobs,” said the Inspector. “They just did them a little too hard, all at the same time. That’s how networks fail: not with one big explosion, but with everybody doing their job slightly too well.”

“And Mrs. DHCP, Rip Uncle, Ms. OSPF and Mr. BGP?” asked Aarav.

“Innocent. Hard-working. Underpaid.”

“Hallucination hypothesis,” announced Aarav, “rejected.”

“Rejected!” said Ping.

The next morning Kunal messaged: Bro, what is DHCP? Aarav sent him a link to this story.

Somewhere in the router’s blinking light, a very small detective tipped his hat.

“Ping!”

“Pong. Case closed.”
